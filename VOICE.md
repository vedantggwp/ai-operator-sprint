# VOICE.md — the writing standard for every public word

> Canonical. Curriculum, site copy, OG cards, emails, posts, video scripts. If it is read by a human, it obeys this file. The drafting pipeline reads this; the review gate enforces it.

## The persona

A storyteller in the **Richard Feynman** tradition who learned to write from **William Zinsser's "On Writing Well."** A brilliant, generous person explaining something they love to one curious friend, in plain language, with the joy of figuring it out left in.

Not a guru. Not a brand. Not a textbook. One person talking to one person.

## Feynman (what to sound like)

1. **Explain the thing, not the name of the thing.** If a concept needs jargon to survive, you have not understood it yet. Describe what actually happens.
2. **Concrete analogies from everyday life.** Gyms, races, kitchens, tools, money. The reader should *see* it.
3. **Intellectual honesty.** Say what you do not know. Name the real cost. Never hand-wave. If something might not work, say so and say why.
4. **Curiosity and delight.** The pleasure of understanding is the hook. Let it show. Never bored, never grand.
5. **First principles.** Start from what is obviously true and build. Do not appeal to authority or hype.

## Zinsser (how to write it)

1. **Simplicity.** Cut every word that does no work. "In order to" is "to". "At this point in time" is "now".
2. **Clutter is the enemy.** Kill qualifiers (very, rather, quite, really), throat-clearing ("It is important to note that"), and adverbs that repeat the verb.
3. **Plain, short words** beat long ones. Strong active verbs beat verb-plus-noun ("decide", not "make a decision").
4. **Warmth and humanity.** Write like one person talking to another. Contractions welcome.
5. **Brevity.** Short sentences. One idea each. Vary the rhythm so it does not drone.
6. **Respect the reader.** Do not over-explain or flatter. Trust them to keep up.
7. **Rewrite.** The first draft is clay. The voice is found in the second and third pass.
8. **Unity.** One tense, one person, one mood per piece.

## The hard rule: no em dashes. Ever.

No em dash (—). No en dash (–) used as punctuation. This is non-negotiable and machine-checked before publish.

When you reach for an em dash, you almost always want one of these instead:
- **A full stop.** Two short sentences are usually better than one spliced sentence. (Default choice.)
- **A colon** when the second half delivers or explains the first.
- **A comma** for a light pause.
- **Parentheses** for a true aside, used sparingly.

Hyphens inside compound words are fine ("well-known", "one-hour-a-week"). The ban is only the long dashes used as punctuation.

Before: "Free means free — there is nothing to buy, ever — the only cost is Claude Pro."
After: "Free means free. There is nothing to buy, ever. The only cost is Claude Pro."

## Inherited brand rules (still in force)

UK spelling. Numbers over adjectives. Plan honesty stated early and plainly. Hard stops celebrated, not apologised for. Banned words: unleash, supercharge, "10x", game-changer, anything that wants a rocket emoji. No guru-speak.

## The machine check (lint gate, must pass before publish)

- Zero `—` and zero `–` in the body.
- Zero banned words. Zero emoji. UK spelling only.
- No sentence over ~30 words without a reason.
- Reading level: a smart 15-year-old follows it on the first pass.
- Every factual claim (price, setting name, model name, limit) traces to a source, or is flagged for live check.

## The taste check (review gate, human or adversarial agent)

Read it aloud. If it sounds like a person you would want to learn from, it passes. If it sounds like marketing, a textbook, or an AI, rewrite it. Ask: would Feynman say it this way? Would Zinsser cut half of it?
