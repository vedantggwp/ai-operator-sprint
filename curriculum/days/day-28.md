## Lesson


The thing you built is not the thing you're selling. The story of how you built it is.


This sounds backwards. It isn't. What your Day 30 person will remember is not the tool; it's the moment they understood what you're capable of. That understanding comes from hearing the story of someone who noticed a problem, tried something, got it wrong, fixed it, and finished. The tool is just proof that the story is real.


A case study is a story with a structure: the situation before, the thing you tried, the result you got, and what you'd do next. Four parts. Under 300 words. Written in plain English, in the first person, without using the word "innovative" or any of the other words that mean nothing.


The case study has two audiences. One is your Day 30 person, who will read it after the demo if they want to share it. The other is future-you, who will be asked in a meeting, or an interview, or a conversation, "so what have you actually done with AI?" and will need a 2-minute answer that's specific, honest, and interesting. The case study is where you write that answer down while the experience is fresh.


Today you write it once, with Claude as your editor. Then you own it.


## Build


1. **Gather your inputs (1 min).** Open in separate tabs: your `COMMITMENT` file from Day 0, your `DEMO-SPEC`, and your proof page from yesterday. These are the three sources. Don't write from memory when the real record exists.


2. **Run the case study interview (4 min).** In Sprint HQ:


   ```
   Help me write a case study for the AI Operator Sprint.


   Here's the raw material:
   - My original commitment (Day 0): [paste COMMITMENT]
   - What I built (Demo Spec): [paste the "what I'm showing" section]
   - Evidence it worked (from proof page): [paste the "proof it works" bullets]


   Interview me with 3 questions, one at a time:
   1. What was the thing you were doing manually before you built this, and roughly how long did it take?
   2. What was the moment in the sprint where something clicked, when you understood what you were actually building?
   3. If you were telling a colleague about this in two minutes, what would you say?


   After the interview, write a case study with this structure:
   - Headline (one sentence, active voice, no jargon)
   - Situation (what you were doing before, what the cost was)
   - What you tried (the sprint, the tool, the build)
   - Result (specific, measured, honest)
   - What's next (one sentence, genuine)


   Under 300 words. First person. No marketing language. No mention of "AI" in the first sentence.
   ```


3. **Edit for your voice (3 min).** Read the case study aloud. Every sentence you'd stumble reading, rewrite. Every adjective that doesn't add information, cut. Every vague claim ("improved significantly"), replace with a specific one ("down from 20 minutes to about 3"). The case study is done when you can read it in 90 seconds without stumbling.


4. **Save in two places (2 min).** Save as `CASE-STUDY` in `AI-OS`. Also add it to the proof page from yesterday (as a "How it was built" section at the bottom). Now the proof page tells both stories: what it is, and how it got made.

## If it goes wrong


- **The case study sounds like a press release.** Cut everything after "In summary", "Overall", or "This demonstrates". Those are endings of a document no one reads. End with the "what's next" sentence. That's the honest end.
- **I can't answer the "moment it clicked" question.** That's okay. Use: "The thing that surprised me most was..." or "I didn't expect it to work but...". A genuine admission is more compelling than a fabricated epiphany.
- **The result section is vague because I don't have numbers.** Write the honest estimate: "I think it saves me about an hour a week, based on 3 weeks of using it. I'll know more in a month." An acknowledged estimate is better than a precise-sounding number you made up.
- **The case study is too short.** Under 300 words is the constraint, not a minimum. If it makes the case in 200, it's done. Padding to hit a word count is how writing becomes bad writing.
